Project 1 Review - Group 6
Group 6’s Project Link: https://lee-sifu.github.io/Project-1_Cart263/

I got to explore the interactive project created by Group 6. Their game appears to take inspiration from incremental clicking games such as Cookie Clicker and early mobile games from the iPhone 4 era. The main interaction is simple. The player clicks on the screen to gain points. Although the mechanic is minimal, the project highlights interesting ideas about user behaviour and motivation. It shows how even a basic interaction can keep players engaged when a system of points and progress is involved.

One of the first things I noticed was how stripped back the design is. The interface relies mostly on plain colours with occasional background images. This simplicity keeps the focus on the central mechanic of clicking. With very few visual distractions, the player becomes more aware of their own actions and how repetitive the interaction becomes over time. This design choice also reflects the type of early mobile games that relied on very direct and accessible gameplay. A moment that stood out happens when the player reaches 30 clicks. At this point, the message “You are breaking the system” appears shaking on screen. I found this moment surprising because it creates the impression that the player is doing something unintended. In reality, the player is only following the game’s main mechanic. The message feels playful but also slightly confrontational. It raises the question of whether the game is warning the player or encouraging them to continue pushing the limits of the system. Moments like this make the experience feel more interactive because the system appears to react directly to the player’s behaviour. Another notable feature happens when the player reaches 50 clicks. The game automatically restarts, and the player loses their progress. This was one of the most compelling aspects of the experience. The sudden reset creates a sense of frustration that many players will recognise. It reminded me of losing progress in a game after forgetting to save. The reset also exposes how quickly players can become attached to a simple point system. Even when the reward is small, there is still a strong urge to keep clicking and try again. This moment highlights how strongly players respond to systems that measure progress.

From a coding perspective, the project demonstrates how simple logic can create a meaningful interactive experience. The click counter appears to track the number of interactions and trigger specific responses when certain thresholds are reached. Conditional statements likely control when the warning message appears and when the game resets. While the code behind the project is probably not very complex, it effectively shows how basic programming structures can shape player behaviour and expectation. During the presentation, the group was asked whether they would consider adding new features. They responded that they might redesign the project so it works better on mobile devices. They also mentioned the possibility of introducing more interactions beyond clicking. Adding new mechanics could expand the experience while still keeping the core idea of incremental progress. 
Overall, I found the project interesting because it uses a very simple mechanic to explore player persistence and reward systems. By limiting the interaction to repeated clicking, the game encourages players to reflect on why they continue playing. Even with minimal visuals and mechanics, the project succeeds in creating a small but emotionally provoking interactive experience.
